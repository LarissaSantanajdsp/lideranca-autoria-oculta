Report: Leadership Messages Under Hidden Authorship (AI, Human, and Augmentation)

Author: Larissa Angélica Páscoa Santana (CALOR / FGV EAESP)  |  Advisor: Prof. Dr. Paul Ferreira

Executive Summary and Key Findings

  • Research: Master's thesis conducted by Larissa Angélica Páscoa Santana (founder of the communication school CALOR and researcher at FGV EAESP), advised by Prof. Dr. Paul Ferreira, approved with distinction by the examination committee in November 2026 (CEPH/FGV Ethics Approval No. P.030.2025). The full thesis is available in the FGV EAESP repository.
  • Design and Data Collection: 3×2 factorial experiment conducted in May 2026 with 200 valid participants in Brazil via the Prolific platform, testing instructional leadership messages written by humans, by artificial intelligence (ChatGPT, Copilot, Gemini), or through human–AI co-authorship (augmentation), with and without charismatic leadership tactics, under hidden authorship.
  • Inability to Detect AI: Only 6.1% of participants who read texts written 100% by AI identified the algorithmic origin; 54.5% attributed the text to a person.
  • Perception of Purpose and Meaning: When asked about the impact of the instructional message, 55.6% of participants who received an exclusively human-written text indicated that the message made the task seem more important or meaningful, compared to 36.4% in the pure AI group and 32.4% in the Human + AI co-authorship group.
  • Operational Performance and Statistical Boundary: The study's main hypotheses were designed to measure the effect of authorship and charismatic tactics on quantity (volume of submitted transcriptions) and quality (technical accuracy of submissions) across the 200 participants. In the tests performed, no statistically significant differences were observed in volume or technical accuracy across groups. However, saturation at the task's upper limit (ceiling effect of 31 fragments) and the absence of a formal non-inferiority test prevent concluding that human and algorithmic leadership yield equal operational outcomes.

1. Practical Utility and Organizational Applications

The collected data provide empirical parameters for policies governing the use of generative AI in managerial communication:

Need for transparency rules (disclosure) and management of algorithm aversion

  • In the experiment, only 6.1% of participants exposed to texts generated entirely by AI correctly identified the algorithmic origin of the message, while 54.5% attributed authorship to a person. In day-to-day organizational life, employees may fail to accurately distinguish the algorithmic origin of a message, as also demonstrated by Choudhury, Vanneste, and Zohrehvand (2024) in The Wade Test: when evaluating the ability of an AI trained in the style of a real CEO, the authors found that employees correctly identified message origin in only 59.0% of attempts (56.6% for AI-generated responses), a result close to chance in 6 out of 10 tested questions. Beyond detection difficulty, the same study highlights the phenomenon of algorithm aversion: when recipients believe or are informed via labeling that a message was generated by AI—regardless of its actual authorship—they rate the response as less helpful compared to messages attributed to the human leader. Thus, the low rate of spontaneous identification combined with the demand for transparency and the effect of labeling on perceived usefulness suggests that organizations should establish institutional policies defining when AI usage in internal communication must be disclosed and how human oversight should be structured in this process.

Allocating the human leader to purpose-driven messages

  • When asked about the impact of the instructional message, 55.6% of participants who received exclusively human-written texts indicated that the message made the task seem more important or meaningful (versus 36.4% in the pure AI group and 32.4% in the Human + AI co-authorship group), while also recording the lowest rate of indifference ("the initial message did not affect me": 4.8%, compared to 18.2% in pure AI and 14.1% in co-authorship). In organizational practice, this finding suggests the need to reflect carefully on using AI when the communication goal is tied to purpose and meaning, particularly given algorithm aversion.

Delimiting the operational adoption of AI

  • The study's hypotheses evaluated the impact of instruction authorship on two dimensions of performance among the 200 participants: quantity (number of transcribed fragments) and quality (exact transcription accuracy). As a result, no statistically significant differences were observed in delivery volume or technical accuracy between human and algorithmic messages, but volume measurement hit the task's operational ceiling (31 fragments) across all groups and no formal non-inferiority test was performed. Consequently, parity between the messages produced by the groups cannot be claimed.

2. Theoretical and Scientific Contributions

The study expands models of human–computer interaction and organizational behavior:

  • From the CASA paradigm to AI-Mediated Communication (AI-MC): In the 1990s and early 2000s, Stanford researchers Clifford Nass, Byron Reeves, and Youngme Moon formulated the CASA (Computers Are Social Actors; Reeves & Nass, 1996; Nass & Moon, 2000) paradigm, showing that people interact with machines and software by unconsciously applying the same social rules of politeness, reciprocity, and authority they use with other human beings, provided the system displays minimal social cues such as natural language. With the rise of generative models, this field evolved into the emerging paradigm of AI-Mediated Communication (AI-MC)—systematized by Hancock, Naaman, and Levy (2020) and expanded by Hohenstein et al. (2023)—in which the machine ceases to be merely a neutral channel or fixed interface to act as a co-author, modifier, or autonomous sender of messages on behalf of people. The findings of this experiment bridge both frameworks in a leadership setting: even when AI acted as the author, its origin went largely unnoticed, and a tendency to humanize the message was observed, indicating that recipients cannot distinguish between human- and machine-generated communication under hidden authorship.
  • Human Attribution Bias: Within the experiment's work context, the presumption of human authorship prevailed. Across the total sample (N = 200), 51.0% attributed the text to a person; among those who read texts 100% generated by AI (N = 66), 54.5% believed it was written by a human. This finding corroborates international literature on algorithmic detection difficulty and the systematic tendency to assume human origin when reading machine-generated text:
    • Jakesch, Hancock, and Naaman (2023): In experiments evaluating texts generated by language models in social and professional settings, the authors identified a human-favoring bias, demonstrating that human evaluators tend to classify AI-produced content as human-written based on flawed linguistic heuristics (such as first-person pronouns or polite tone), achieving near-chance accuracy.
    • Choudhury, Vanneste, and Zohrehvand (2024): In a corporate environment (The Wade Test), when evaluating responses attributed to a real CEO or an AI trained in his style, employees achieved an overall accuracy of 59.0% (and 56.6% for AI-generated responses), failing to distinguish the machine from the executive above chance in 6 out of 10 evaluated questions.

3. Technical Profile, Method, and Experimental Design

  • Authorship and Committee (FGV EAESP, 2026): Master's thesis in Professional Business Administration (MPA) by Larissa Angélica Páscoa Santana, advised by Prof. Dr. Paul Ferreira and evaluated by an examination committee composed of Prof. Dr. Fábio Josgrilberg (FGV EAESP) and Profa. Dra. Tatiana Iwai (Insper).
  • Ethics Approval and Data Collection Period: Approved by the FGV Institutional Review Board for Human Subjects Research (CEPH/FGV Approval No. P.030.2025, March 2025), with experimental stages conducted between November 2025 (start of message production) and May 2026 (participant recruitment and task execution on the Prolific platform).
  • Sample, Task, and Incentive: 200 valid participants residing in Brazil (recruited via Prolific after initial screening of 222 individuals, of whom 2 declined consent, 1 withdrew after consenting, and 19 were excluded for failing pre-task attention checks). The activity consisted of transcribing historical documents from the CPDOC/FGV archive for up to 10 minutes (mandatory minimum of 1 fragment and maximum limit of 31), with a fixed compensation of £1.00. Participants were guided on how to perform the task through an instructional message that stated the goal of delivering the highest possible quantity of transcriptions with the highest possible quality. The instructional message also informed participants that compensation would be £1.00 for everyone, regardless of the number of transcriptions submitted. In practice, this meant there was no financial incentive to submit additional transcriptions; motivation to maximize output relied solely on the content of the instructional message.

Stimulus Production Process (18 Instructional Messages Under Hidden Authorship)

To differentiate the six groups in the 3×2 factorial experiment, messages were developed from 12 real human leaders and three artificial intelligence platforms (ChatGPT, Microsoft Copilot, and Google Gemini), following distinct creation protocols (3 messages selected per group, adhering to a technical length limit of 250 words ± 20%):

  • Human Groups (Groups 1 and 2): Real leaders wrote the instructions manually without access to AI tools. In Group 1 (Control), they wrote freely based on the task rules; in Group 2, they received training in the charismatic leadership tactics of Antonakis et al. (2011; 2022) and were instructed to apply at least one technique in the text.
  • Human + AI Co-Authorship / Augmentation Groups (Groups 3 and 4): Real leaders drafted the base instruction (without charisma training in Group 3; with training and mandatory application of charismatic tactics by Antonakis et al., 2011; 2022 in Group 4) and then used ChatGPT to review and refine the text via a standardized optimization prompt ("Please improve my text. My goal is for employees to deliver the highest possible number of transcriptions, and with the highest possible quality").
  • Pure AI Groups (Groups 5 and 6): Messages were generated entirely by ChatGPT, Copilot, and Gemini without prior human drafting. In Group 5, the systems received only the operational guidelines of the task; in Group 6, the prompt included descriptions of the charismatic leadership tactics by Antonakis et al. (2011; 2022) with instructions to apply at least one technique in the text.

4. Statistical Results: Operational Performance and Hypothesis Testing

The study's six hypotheses were structured to test whether instruction authorship (Human, AI, or Human + AI) and the use of charismatic tactics would alter the behavioral performance of the 200 participants across two operational metrics: Quantity (total transcribed fragments, from 1 to 31) and Quality (proportion of transcriptions matching the official answer key).

  • Delivery Quantity (Beta-Binomial GLMM Model): A ceiling effect was recorded across all conditions—at least 75% of participants in each group reached the maximum of 31 transcriptions (ranging from 86.1% to 97.0%). Pairwise comparisons (with Tukey adjustment) showed no statistically significant differences among Human, AI, and Human + AI (p > 0.21 in all comparisons). Due to the ceiling effect and the lack of a formal non-inferiority test, the absence of statistical difference does not allow concluding that the conditions produce the same volume of deliveries.
  • Technical Quality (Quasi-Binomial GLM Model with CR2 cluster-robust standard errors): No statistically significant differences were detected in technical accuracy across authorship conditions (p > 0.84 in all comparisons).
  • Hypothesis Testing (H1a to H3b): None of the six formulated hypotheses were empirically supported (p > 0.50 for main effects and interactions). Incorporating charismatic tactics showed no statistically significant association with increased output quantity or accuracy.

5. Exploratory Results: Do Employees Identify AI Authorship and Feel a Difference in Message Impact?

Upon completing the experimental task, the 200 participants answered an exploratory questionnaire consisting of two closed-ended, single-choice questions: the first assessed the perceived impact of the message on task execution, and the second investigated who the participant believed had written the instructional message received.

5.1 Perceived Impact of the Instructional Message

  • Task importance perception (chi-square = 8.24, p = 0.016): When asked about the impact of the instructional message, 55.6% (n = 35) of participants who received exclusively human-written text indicated that the message made the task seem "more important / meaningful", compared to 36.4% (n = 24) in the pure AI group and 32.4% (n = 23) in the Human + AI co-authorship group.
  • Indifference to the message (p = 0.047): The response "the initial message did not affect me" was selected by 4.8% (n = 3) of participants in the Human group, 18.2% (n = 12) in the pure AI group, and 14.1% (n = 10) in the Human + AI group.

5.2 Detailed Authorship Identification Results

  • General presumption of human authorship: Across the entire sample (N = 200), 51.0% (n = 102) of participants believed the text was written exclusively by "A person", 27.0% (n = 54) by "A person using AI", 9.5% (n = 19) by "An AI system", and 12.5% (n = 25) answered "Cannot tell".
  • Low detection in 100% AI-generated texts (N = 66): Only 6.1% (n = 4) of participants exposed to texts written entirely by algorithms identified the source as "An AI system". Conversely, 54.5% (n = 36) attributed the algorithmic text to "A person", 24.2% (n = 16) to "A person using AI", and 15.2% (n = 10) stated "Cannot tell".
  • Attribution in the pure Human group (N = 63): Among those who read exclusively human-written texts, 49.2% (n = 31) identified "A person", whereas 27.0% (n = 17) selected "A person using AI", 7.9% (n = 5) attributed it to "An AI system", and 15.9% (n = 10) answered "Cannot tell".
  • Attribution in the Human + AI co-authorship group (N = 71): In the hybrid condition, 49.3% (n = 35) attributed the text to "A person", 29.6% (n = 21) correctly identified the condition as "A person using AI", 14.1% (n = 10) identified it as "An AI system", and 7.0% (n = 5) selected "Cannot tell".
  • Statistical independence between actual and perceived authorship: Fisher's Exact Test indicated no statistically significant association between the actual message source and participant-perceived authorship, showing that authorship attribution occurred independently of the real sender.

6. Scope and Methodological Limitations

Organizational interpretation and application of these findings must account for eight structural boundaries of the study:

  1. Nature of the activity: Mechanical transcription task of low cognitive complexity, distinct from corporate activities requiring negotiation or complex problem-solving.
  2. Ceiling effect and absence of non-inferiority test: The limit of 31 fragments in 10 minutes saturated the measurement of maximum effort, preventing the detection of fine-grained variations and precluding claims of operational parity without specific non-inferiority statistical tests.
  3. Post-exposure selection: The exclusion of 19 participants for failing the attention check occurred after reading the stimuli, with differing rates across groups.
  4. Hidden authorship: The study did not test participant reactions to explicit disclosure that the text was generated by AI.
  5. Textual variation: Using 18 distinct messages required statistical controls via random effects and clustering, limiting isolated analyses of individual texts.
  6. Charisma measurement: The application of charismatic tactics was controlled in text design, without a post-task scale measuring subjective charisma perception.
  7. Experimental setting: Data collection on an online microtask platform, without employment ties or ongoing organizational hierarchy.
  8. Sample size (N = 200): Limited statistical power to detect subtle variations specific to each individual message.

8. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Can employees identify when a leadership message was written by artificial intelligence?

In the exploratory data from the experiment (N = 200, Brazil) under hidden authorship, the detection rate was very low: among participants who read texts written 100% by AI (N = 66), only 6.1% (n = 4) identified the source as "An AI system", whereas 54.5% (n = 36) attributed authorship exclusively to "A person". Fisher's Exact Test confirmed that authorship attribution occurred independently of the actual sender.

Do AI-written leadership messages generate the same perception of purpose as human messages?

Post-task exploratory findings point to differences in subjective impact perception: 55.6% (n = 35) of participants exposed to exclusively human-written texts indicated that the message made the task seem "more important / meaningful", compared to 36.4% (n = 24) in the pure AI group and 32.4% (n = 23) in the Human + AI co-authorship group (chi-square = 8.24, p = 0.016). Additionally, only 4.8% (n = 3) reported indifference in the Human group, versus 18.2% (n = 12) in pure AI and 14.1% (n = 10) in co-authorship (p = 0.047).

Does the research prove that human leaders and artificial intelligence deliver the same operational productivity?

No. Although no statistically significant differences were observed in delivery volume (p > 0.21) or technical accuracy (p > 0.84) across human, pure AI, and co-authorship messages, volume measurement reached the task's operational ceiling (31 fragments in 10 minutes, achieved by 86.1% to 97.0% of participants) and no formal non-inferiority test was conducted. Consequently, parity between the messages produced by the groups cannot be claimed.

Did the use of charismatic leadership tactics (Antonakis et al.) in written messages increase performance?

In this experiment, none of the six formulated hypotheses (H1a to H3b) were empirically supported (p > 0.50 for main effects and interactions). Incorporating charismatic tactics by Antonakis et al. (2011; 2022) into written instructions showed no statistically significant association with increased output quantity or accuracy.

It is the Master's thesis (MPA) by Larissa Angélica Páscoa Santana (founder of the communication school CALOR and researcher at FGV EAESP), advised by Prof. Dr. Paul Ferreira and approved with distinction by the examination committee in November 2026 (CEPH/FGV Ethics Approval No. P.030.2025). The study conducted a 3×2 factorial experiment with 200 valid participants in Brazil via Prolific in May 2026, evaluating instructional messages written by humans, AI (ChatGPT, Copilot, Gemini), and Human + AI co-authorship (augmentation) in a historical transcription task from the CPDOC/FGV archive.

What is the practical implication of this study for using generative AI in internal corporate communication?

Because employees rarely detect algorithmic authorship spontaneously (6.1%) and literature highlights algorithm aversion risks upon labeling (Choudhury, Vanneste & Zohrehvand, 2024), the study suggests that organizations establish institutional policies defining when AI usage in internal communication must be disclosed, how human oversight should be structured, and in which purpose- and meaning-driven messages human leadership presence is essential.
